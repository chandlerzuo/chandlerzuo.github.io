Every saver eventually faces a deceptively simple choice. Hand the government $100 today in exchange for a thirty-year Treasury bond, and you lock in a coupon for a generation. Or keep the money on a short leash, parking it in four-week Treasury bills and rolling them over, month after month, at whatever rate prevails. The first is a marriage; the second, a string of dates. Which leaves you richer?

The honest answer, as with most things in finance, is "it depends on how long you wait". To put numbers on that intuition, we assembled thirty-six years of daily Treasury data, from 1990 to the present, and asked what each strategy would actually have earned. We then built a model of the yield curve to ask what a bond bought today, at a coupon of around 5.35%, is likely to return in the years ahead. Two exercises, one lesson: the long bond is a reward for patience, and a punishment for the impatient. The data and code behind everything below are available in [this repository](https://github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/treasury_analysis).

**A note on notation**

Throughout, an investor buys at time $$0$$ and sells at horizon $$h$$ (in years), choosing $$h \in \{1,3,5,10\}$$. The bond has face value $$F = 100$$, matures in $$T = 30$$ years, and pays coupons $$m = 2$$ times a year. Because we always buy at par, the annual coupon rate equals the prevailing thirty-year par yield at purchase, $$c = y_{30}(0)$$, and each coupon payment is $$cF/m$$. We write $$y(\tau)$$ for the annualised yield on a zero of maturity $$\tau$$, and reserve $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ and $$R^{\text{bill}}_h$$ for the annualised returns of the two strategies over horizon $$h$$.

**What history paid**

Consider first the ledger of the past. For every year since 1990 we imagine buying a fresh thirty-year bond, collecting its coupons at dates $$t_k = k/m$$, and selling the residual bond after $$h$$ years - then comparing the annualised return with that of the rolling-bill saver over the same window.

The mechanics are worth spelling out, because the whole argument rests on them. At the sale date the bond has become an ordinary $$(T-h)$$-year instrument, and we value it by discounting its remaining cash flows along the yield curve $$y_h(\cdot)$$ prevailing on that date. Writing $$u_k = t_k - h$$ for the time from sale to each surviving cash flow, the sale price is

$$
P_h \;=\; \sum_{t_k > h} \frac{cF/m}{\left(1 + \tfrac{y_h(u_k)}{m}\right)^{m u_k}} \;+\; \frac{F}{\left(1 + \tfrac{y_h(T-h)}{m}\right)^{m (T-h)}}.
$$

The bond's realised return is then the internal rate of return - the single rate $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ that equates the purchase price with the present value of every coupon collected while held plus the sale proceeds:

$$
P_0 \;=\; \sum_{t_k \le h} \frac{cF/m}{\left(1 + R^{\text{bond}}_h\right)^{t_k}} \;+\; \frac{P_h}{\left(1 + R^{\text{bond}}_h\right)^{h}}, \qquad P_0 = F = 100.
$$

The bill-roller's arithmetic is humbler. Over horizon $$h$$ the saver reinvests $100 into successive four-week bills, $$n$$ of them, each compounding at its prevailing coupon-equivalent yield $$y^{\text{bill}}_j$$ over an interval $$\Delta = 28/365$$ of a year. Terminal wealth and the annualised return are

$$
W_h \;=\; F \prod_{j=1}^{n} \left(1 + y^{\text{bill}}_j \, \Delta\right), \qquad R^{\text{bill}}_h \;=\; \left(\frac{W_h}{F}\right)^{1/h} - 1.
$$

Averaged across all starting years, the bond won at every horizon. Held for a single year it returned 6.4%, against 2.8% for bills; held for ten, 6.8% against 2.3%. The gap - some three-and-a-half to four-and-a-half percentage points a year - is the premium the market pays those willing to bear duration risk. Laid side by side, the two distributions tell the story at a glance: the bond's box sits above the bill's at every horizon, but its whiskers - the span of plausible outcomes - collapse dramatically as the holding period lengthens, while the bills plod along, reliably dull.

That premium, however, comes wrapped in volatility, and the wrapping matters more over short horizons than long ones. The one-year bond return had a standard deviation of a stomach-churning 15%; the ten-year figure was under 3%. The reason is visible in the two equations above. Over a single year, the sale price $$P_h$$ dominates the cash-flow stream, and $$P_h$$ is exquisitely sensitive to the yield curve on one particular day. Stretch the holding period, and the steady drip of coupons increasingly outweighs the vagaries of that single sale price.

To judge whether the extra return justified the extra risk, we compute a Sharpe ratio on the bond's *excess* return over bills. For a given horizon, let $$e_i = R^{\text{bond}}_{h,i} - R^{\text{bill}}_{h,i}$$ be the excess return for start year $$i$$, across $$N$$ such years. The ratio is the mean excess return per unit of its own volatility:

$$
\mathrm{Sharpe}_h \;=\; \frac{\bar e}{s_e}, \qquad \bar e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i, \qquad s_e = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(e_i - \bar e\right)^2}.
$$

The result is striking. The Sharpe ratio climbs from a feeble 0.24 at one year to 0.51 at three, 0.79 at five, and a commanding 1.94 at ten. Put differently, an investor flipping in and out of long bonds annually was taking a coin-flip; one who bought and held for a decade was running a near-sure thing. Across our sample the bond beat bills 61% of the time over one year, but 93% of the time over ten. Held for a full decade, the bond never once posted a negative annualised return - its worst ten-year outcome still crept in above zero.

The losses, when they came, were concentrated and instructive. Bonds bought in the mid-2010s, at the fag-end of the great disinflation, were later mauled by the 2022 rate spike: a bond bought in 2022 and sold a year later lost 30% at an annual rate. Duration, so generous in a falling-rate world, turns vicious when rates climb.

**A rival suitor**

Before crowning the bond, it is worth introducing a third contestant that few savers can ignore: the stock market. Running the same start-year experiment against the total return of the S&P 500 humbles the bond considerably. Equities beat the long bond in the majority of historical windows, and did so on a risk-adjusted basis at most horizons too. Only at the ten-year mark did the bond's dependability begin to rival stocks' higher but jumpier returns.

Applying the same excess-return Sharpe measure to both assets makes the ranking explicit. Equities out-Sharpe the bond at one, three and five years; only at the ten-year mark does the bond's metronomic reliability edge ahead of stocks' higher but jumpier returns.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part1_sharpe_bond_vs_sp500_bars.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

There is a large caveat stamped across all of this. The years from 1990 to 2026 were, for bonds, a golden age: yields fell for most of three decades, and falling yields mean rising $$P_h$$. History, in this case, had a thumb on the scale. Which is why a backward-looking ledger is not enough.

**What the future might pay**

To peer forward, we need a model of the entire yield curve, and then a model of how that curve moves. For the first we use the Nelson-Siegel parameterisation, which compresses the curve on any given day into three interpretable numbers. The yield at maturity $$\tau$$ is

$$
y(\tau) \;=\; \beta_0 \;+\; \beta_1 \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda \tau}}{\lambda \tau} \;+\; \beta_2 \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda \tau}}{\lambda \tau} - e^{-\lambda \tau} \right), \qquad \lambda = 1.37.
$$

The three coefficients have clean readings: $$\beta_0$$ is the *level* (its loading is constant, so it shifts the whole curve), $$\beta_1$$ is the *slope* (its loading decays from 1 to 0, so it tilts the short end), and $$\beta_2$$ is the *curvature* (its loading is hump-shaped, so it bows the middle). With the decay parameter fixed at $$\lambda = 1.37$$, the three $$\beta$$'s are estimated by ordinary least squares against observed yields on each month-end, fitting the curve to within about 9.5 basis points. Fitted month by month across thirty-six years, the three factors trace the economic history of the period: the level $$\beta_0$$ drifts down through the long disinflation and back up after 2021, the slope $$\beta_1$$ dips negative when the curve inverts before recessions, and the fit error stays a tight handful of basis points throughout.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part2_ns_components_fit.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

For the second model, we let the triple $$\mathbf f_t = (\beta_{0,t}, \beta_{1,t}, \beta_{2,t})^\top$$ evolve as a first-order vector autoregression:

$$
\mathbf f_t \;=\; \boldsymbol\mu \;+\; \boldsymbol\Phi \, \mathbf f_{t-1} \;+\; \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma).
$$

Here $$\boldsymbol\mu$$ is a drift, $$\boldsymbol\Phi$$ a $$3\times 3$$ matrix of persistence and cross-effects, and $$\boldsymbol\Sigma$$ the covariance of the monthly shocks. The system is stationary - the eigenvalues of $$\boldsymbol\Phi$$ have moduli 0.91, 0.98 and 0.98, all below one - so simulated curves are pulled gently back towards their long-run average rather than wandering off to infinity. From today's estimated factors we draw $$S = 20{,}000$$ monthly Gaussian paths out to ten years. Each path implies a future curve $$y_h(\cdot)$$, hence a sale price $$P_h$$ via the pricing equation above, and a sequence of short rates that the bill-roller earns - and thus, per path, a realised $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ and $$R^{\text{bill}}_h$$.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part2_yield_fan.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

Simulated forward, the model does not produce a single curve but a distribution of them. The fans below show the 5th-to-95th and 25th-to-75th percentile range of the whole yield curve at each horizon: near-term uncertainty is modest, but by five and ten years the plausible band widens to several percentage points at every maturity, and the mean-reverting pull nudges the median curve back towards its historical shape.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part2_simulated_curve_distribution.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

The map echoes history with uncanny fidelity. A thirty-year bond bought today, at a coupon near 5.35%, has a median projected return of roughly 6% at every horizon - the coupon, in effect, anchoring the outcome. But the spread around that median tells the real story. Over one year, the middle 90% of outcomes ran from a loss of 10% to a gain of 28%; there was a 29% chance of simply losing money. Over ten years, the same band narrowed to between 4% and 9%, and the probability of a loss fell to nil.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part2_irr_distributions.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

The forward-looking Sharpe ratio, computed over the simulated paths as

$$
\mathrm{Sharpe}^{\text{fwd}}_h \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[R^{\text{bond}}_h - R^{\text{bill}}_h\right]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\!\left(R^{\text{bond}}_h - R^{\text{bill}}_h\right)}},
$$

climbs from 0.26 at one year to 1.24 at ten, tracing the same upward arc the historical record showed - only now derived entirely from forward simulation rather than the past. The probability of beating rolling bills rises in step, from 57% to 89%.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/treasury_analysis/outputs/part2_forward_sharpe.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

In both the rear-view mirror and the crystal ball, the message is identical. Buying the long bond for a year is a bet on the direction of interest rates dressed up as an investment. Buying it for a decade is a bet on the coupon, and the coupon almost always pays.

**The moral**

None of this is investment advice, and the model wears its limitations openly. It ignores taxes, trading costs and the settlement niceties of real bond markets. Its prices are theoretical, spun from a fitted curve rather than a trading screen. Above all, the Gaussian shocks $$\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t$$ assume a well-behaved, bell-shaped world, and so the model politely understates the fat tails - the 2022s and 2008s - that give bondholders their worst nights.

Yet the central finding survives every caveat, precisely because it rests on the arithmetic of $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ rather than on prophecy. Time is the long bond's ally. The coupon is a certainty; the sale price $$P_h$$ is a gamble; and the longer you hold, the more the certainty swamps the gamble. For the saver who can sit still, the thirty-year bond is less a leap of faith than a slow, compounding act of patience. For the one who cannot, it is best left alone.

**TECHNICAL DETAILS**

The data are daily Treasury par-yield curves (1990-2026, tenors from one month to thirty years) and four-week bill rates, taken from the U.S. Department of the Treasury. The thirty-year par yield was discontinued between February 2002 and February 2006; on those dates the Nelson-Siegel fit $$y(\tau)$$ interpolates the curve. Bond returns $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ solve the internal-rate-of-return equation above, with coupons treated as cash and the residual bond sold at price $$P_h$$ off the fitted curve; bill returns $$R^{\text{bill}}_h$$ are the realised compound growth of the four-week roll. The historical Sharpe ratio $$\mathrm{Sharpe}_h$$ is the mean of the excess series $$\{e_i\}$$ divided by its sample standard deviation, computed across start years by horizon.

For the forward-looking exercise, the Nelson-Siegel factors $$\mathbf f_t$$ were fitted to each month-end at fixed $$\lambda = 1.37$$ (Diebold-Li) and modelled as the stationary VAR(1) above, then simulated for $$S = 20{,}000$$ monthly Gaussian paths from today's factor state. Each path yields a future curve, a sale price $$P_h$$ and a short-rate path, and thus a distribution of $$R^{\text{bond}}_h$$ and $$R^{\text{bill}}_h$$. This is a real-world ("physical measure") projection of likely returns, not an arbitrage-free pricing exercise. Data and full source code are available at [github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/treasury_analysis](https://github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/treasury_analysis).
