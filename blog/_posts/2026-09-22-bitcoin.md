title: Bitcoin's Quiet Conversion - When Did It Actually Become a Macro Asset

When March 2020 arrived, bitcoin fell alongside everything else. The lesson drawn at the time was that the asset's founding promise - a store of value untethered from central banks and the businesses they backstop - had failed its first real examination. The IMF later [put numbers to the intuition](https://doi.org/10.5089/9781616358068.065), calculating that spillovers between bitcoin and American share prices had risen by 12 to 16 percentage points after the pandemic began. A cottage industry of commentary settled on a tidy story: covid-19 was the moment bitcoin became a macroeconomic asset, a leveraged bet on the Nasdaq with worse manners.

The story is nearly right, and wrong about the date. Bitcoin's relationship with the stockmarket did change once, decisively, and has never changed back. But it changed in August 2019 - seven months before the pandemic, and within weeks of the Federal Reserve's first rate cut in a decade. The covid crash did not convert bitcoin. It arrived to find the conversion already complete.

Three findings follow from looking at the data this way. Bitcoin's equity correlation broke once, in 2019, and its *beta* broke separately six years later, in 2025 - and the gap between those two dates is instructive rather than contradictory. Its correlation with gold has never broken at all, which is awkward for the digital-gold thesis. And the one relationship that has genuinely reversed is with the dollar: since February 2018 bitcoin has reliably risen when the dollar falls, which is the debasement trade finally behaving as advertised.

**Two conversions, six years apart**

Split bitcoin's history at that date and the two halves are different assets. Before August 2019 its correlation with American equities averaged 0.04 - statistically indistinguishable from an asset that had never heard of the Federal Reserve. After, it averages 0.30. A formal test for structural change, run without being told where to look, finds exactly one break in fourteen years of weekly returns, and puts it there. March 2020 does not register.

Yet anyone tracking bitcoin's *beta* - the standard measure of how much an asset moves when the market moves - would have seen nothing happen in 2019 at all. Beta stayed near 0.6 for another six years, and only jumped, to 1.34, in July 2025.

Both facts are true, and the reason they can be is the most useful thing in this study. A beta is not a single quantity. It is a correlation multiplied by a ratio of volatilities: how reliably two assets move together, times how violently one of them moves relative to the other. Bitcoin's correlation with equities rose sharply in 2019. Its volatility, meanwhile, was collapsing - from roughly fifteen times the stockmarket's in 2014 to about three times today. For six years the two effects cancelled, and beta sat still while the relationship underneath it transformed.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. In November 2021, at the peak of the last cycle, bitcoin's beta hit 1.97, the highest it has ever been. Read on its own, that looks like the moment of maximum entanglement with the macroeconomy. It was nothing of the sort: the correlation then was a modest 0.28, and the high beta was almost entirely an artefact of bitcoin thrashing about seven times harder than the market. Today's *lower* beta reflects far more genuine co-movement. The number fell while the exposure rose.

**A quarrel with two right answers**

The 2019 date also settles an argument in which both sides had a point. BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager, published [a paper in September 2025](https://www.blackrock.com/us/individual/literature/whitepaper/bitcoin-a-unique-diversifier.pdf) observing that bitcoin's correlation with the S&P 500 over the previous decade averaged only 0.2, and that the spikes were "short-term in nature". The IMF, examining the pandemic, found substantial coupling. Neither was mistaken. A ten-year average straddles a regime boundary and reports the blend; a crisis window reports one side of it. Averaging across a structural break describes neither of the things being averaged.

It is worth saying what did *not* happen in 2019. Bitcoin's correlation with the Nasdaq-100 specifically shows no significant break at any point - it has hovered near 0.17 throughout. Whatever bitcoin became, it did not become a technology stock. It became sensitive to the broad market, which is a different and duller thing.

**The dog that has never barked**

One relationship has been conspicuously, almost defiantly stable. Bitcoin's correlation with gold is 0.07 across thirteen years, and the same test finds no break in it anywhere - not in 2019, not in 2020, not now. For an asset marketed relentlessly as digital gold, this is the most damning number in the dataset. Bitcoin has spent fourteen years failing to behave like bullion, with remarkable consistency.

The dollar is where bitcoin has genuinely switched sides, and this is the one place the popular story about bitcoin turns out to be right. Its correlation with the broad trade-weighted dollar averaged a mildly *positive* +0.07 up to the end of 2017. Since February 2018 it has averaged -0.23, and the sign flip is a statistically significant break, not a drift.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/bitcoin_macro/outputs/figures/essay_dollar.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

The magnitude is economically serious. Regressing weekly bitcoin returns on the dollar since February 2018 gives a slope of -2.91, meaning a 1% weekly fall in the dollar has been worth roughly 2.9% on bitcoin. A one-standard-deviation weekly move in the dollar - about 0.7% - maps to some 2% on bitcoin in the opposite direction.

The lower panel is the more persuasive evidence, because a correlation coefficient can be manufactured by a handful of wild weeks and a monotone staircase cannot. Sort the 451 weeks since February 2018 into five buckets by how the dollar moved, and bitcoin's average return declines without interruption across all five: +3.25% in the weeks the dollar fell hardest, then +1.45%, +0.30%, -0.86%, and -2.24% in the weeks it rose hardest.

Nor is this merely the risk-on trade wearing a different hat. Put the dollar and the stockmarket in the same regression and both survive: the dollar loading is -1.75 with a *t* of -2.6, alongside an equity beta of +0.58 with a *t* of +3.0. Bitcoin's dollar sensitivity is its own thing, not an echo of its equity sensitivity.

**What, then, is it like?**

If bitcoin is not gold and not a technology stock, the natural question is whether anything in financial history has behaved like it. We compared every phase of bitcoin's life against every rolling window of 65 assets going back to 1926 - American industry portfolios, metals, commodities, equity indices - and, separately, against twelve national stockmarkets reaching back to 1957. Some forty thousand candidate windows in all.

The answer is no. In every regime, at every frequency, bitcoin's closest historical match is *worse* than the closest match a randomly chosen asset finds in the same panel. Bitcoin sits outside a century of financial experience. This is a stronger claim than it sounds, and it survives the obvious objection that the panel is too tame: adding emerging markets and six extra decades does not change it.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/bitcoin_macro/outputs/figures/essay_analogue_paths.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

Its *nearest* relatives are instructive anyway, and they are nobody's first guess. In bitcoin's violent years the closest matches are crude oil after the 2020 collapse, natural gas, and silver during the Hunt brothers' squeeze of 1978-80: things that move enormously and pay no attention to the stockmarket. Widen the net to national markets and the closest relative to bitcoin's first decade is the Turkish stockmarket between 1989 and 2003, whose normalised path traces bitcoin's more faithfully than any other pairing we found. An asset held partly as an escape from monetary debasement turns out to resemble the equity market of a country whose savers were doing precisely that, for precisely that reason. For the years since 2020, the best match is American car makers - a high-beta cyclical, which is roughly what the correlation evidence says bitcoin has become.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/master/codes/bitcoin_macro/outputs/figures/essay_analogue_features.png" width="600" style="display:block; margin:1.5em auto;" />

The second chart is the necessary corrective to the first. Stripping out amplitude is exactly what makes those shapes rhyme, and the matched windows agree closely on volatility and drawdown because those are the dimensions the search is looking at. The revealing gaps lie elsewhere. Crude oil in 2020 shared bitcoin's volatility but not remotely its returns. Most tellingly, silver in 1978-80 matches bitcoin on volatility, skew and Sharpe ratio - and then carries a 0.74 correlation with gold, against bitcoin's 0.04. The one historical asset that behaved like bitcoin genuinely *was* a monetary metal. Bitcoin is not.

**The moral**

Two cautions are in order. The July 2025 shift in beta rests on a year of data, and correlations rise mechanically in falling markets - bitcoin's return over that window has been negative, so part of it may be the ordinary arithmetic of a drawdown rather than a change of character. And bitcoin is the survivor of a large cohort of dead cryptocurrencies, which flatters every performance statistic computed from its history.

The 2019 break is the sturdier finding, and its timing invites a thought. August 2019 was not a crisis. It was the month the Federal Reserve began cutting rates for the first time since 2008, a few weeks before it was forced back into the repo market to keep the plumbing working. Bitcoin's conversion into a macro asset did not begin with a crash. It began with easier money - which, for an asset whose entire pitch is the scarcity of its own supply, is a rather awkward origin story.

Bitcoin is growing more correlated with the assets it was invented to escape, while growing less volatile than anything in its own past. For something that spent its adolescence insisting it was different, maturity looks a lot like becoming ordinary.

**ANALYSIS DETAILS**

*What follows is the technical appendix: the estimator, the identity the essay leans on, the measure of similarity, the caveats, and how to reproduce all of it. Data and code: [github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/bitcoin_macro](https://github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/bitcoin_macro).*

**Notation.** $$r^{B}_t$$ and $$r^{M}_t$$ are weekly log returns on bitcoin and on the American equity market, the latter the [Fama-French market factor](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html). Write $$\rho_t$$ for their correlation, $$\sigma_{B,t}$$ and $$\sigma_{M,t}$$ for their conditional volatilities, and $$s_t \equiv \sigma_{B,t}/\sigma_{M,t}$$ for the volatility ratio. A candidate break date is $$c$$; the sample has $$n$$ observations, $$k$$ regressors, and $$q$$ coefficients permitted to break. Returns run January 2013 to September 2026, Friday to Friday.

**The identity.** Everything in the first half of this piece rests on

$$
\beta_t \;=\; \rho_t \, \frac{\sigma_{B,t}}{\sigma_{M,t}} \;=\; \rho_t\, s_t ,
$$

and on decomposing a change in beta between two periods into its two channels plus an interaction,

$$
\underbrace{\rho_1 s_1 - \rho_0 s_0}_{\Delta \beta}
\;=\; \underbrace{(\rho_1 - \rho_0)\,s_0}_{\text{correlation}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\rho_0\,(s_1 - s_0)}_{\text{volatility}}
\;+\; \underbrace{(\rho_1 - \rho_0)(s_1 - s_0)}_{\text{interaction}} .
$$

In late 2012, $$\beta = 1.18$$ from $$\rho = 0.19$$ and $$s = 6.2$$. In mid-2026, $$\beta = 1.78$$ from $$\rho = 0.52$$ and $$s = 3.4$$. In November 2021 the record $$\beta = 1.97$$ came from $$\rho = 0.28$$ and $$s = 7.11$$, so the volatility channel accounts for essentially all of that spike.

**Testing correlation rather than beta.** Because $$\sigma_{B}$$ fell threefold over the sample, a test on $$\beta$$ and a test on $$\rho$$ answer different questions. To isolate $$\rho$$, divide each series by its own exponentially-weighted volatility,

$$
\tilde r^{B}_t = \frac{r^{B}_t}{\hat\sigma_{B,t}}, \qquad
\tilde r^{M}_t = \frac{r^{M}_t}{\hat\sigma_{M,t}}, \qquad
\hat\sigma^2_{i,t} = (1-\lambda) \sum_{j \ge 0} \lambda^{j} \left(r_{i,t-1-j}\right)^2 ,
$$

with $$\lambda = 2^{-1/26}$$, a twenty-six-week half-life. Both standardised series have unit variance, so the slope of $$\tilde r^{B}$$ on $$\tilde r^{M}$$ *is* the correlation, and a break in that slope is a break in $$\rho$$.


**The break test.** The full model has six regressors and an $$R^2$$ near 0.03, so letting all six coefficients break costs six degrees of freedom for almost no gain in fit and the test never rejects. We therefore use the [*partial* structural change model](https://doi.org/10.2307/2998540) of Bai and Perron, splitting coefficients into a subset $$S$$ that may break and a remainder held fixed:

$$
\tilde r^{B}_t \;=\; x_t'\beta \;+\; \left(x_{t,S}\,\mathbf{1}\{t \ge c\}\right)'\delta \;+\; \varepsilon_t ,
\qquad S = \{\text{intercept},\, \tilde r^{M}\}, \qquad q = |S| = 2 .
$$

Under $$H_0 : \delta = 0$$ the usual statistic compares restricted and unrestricted residual sums of squares, and because the break date is unknown we take its supremum over admissible dates:

$$
F(c) \;=\; \frac{\left(\mathrm{SSR}_r - \mathrm{SSR}_u(c)\right)/q}{\mathrm{SSR}_u(c)/(n-k-q)},
\qquad
F^{\sup} \;=\; \max_{c \,\in\, [\tau n,\, (1-\tau)n]} F(c),
\qquad \hat c = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_c F(c) ,
$$

with trimming $$\tau$$ set so that no segment is shorter than twenty-six weeks.

**Critical values.** [Andrews's asymptotic distribution](https://doi.org/10.2307/2951764) for $$F^{\sup}$$ assumes homoskedastic errors, which is untenable here: a [GJR-GARCH](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb05128.x) fit puts the Student-$$t$$ degrees of freedom near 3.5, and the asymmetry parameter is *negative* for bitcoin against $$+0.42$$ for equities - good news raises bitcoin's volatility more than bad news, the reverse of the equity convention. We therefore bootstrap the threshold with Rademacher weights, which impose the null exactly while preserving conditional heteroskedasticity. For $$b = 1, \dots, B$$ with $$B = 399$$,

$$
y^{*(b)}_t = x_t'\hat\beta + \hat\varepsilon_t\, \eta^{(b)}_t ,
\qquad
\eta^{(b)}_t \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}
\begin{cases} +1 & \text{with probability } 1/2 \\ -1 & \text{with probability } 1/2 \end{cases}
\qquad
\hat p = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{ F^{\sup(b)} \ge F^{\sup} \right\} .
$$

Multiple breaks follow by applying this sequentially: test the whole sample, split at $$\hat c$$ if it rejects, and recurse until no segment rejects at 5%.

**Results.** One break in the equity correlation, at 2019-08-16, with $$F^{\sup} = 7.37$$ and $$\hat p = 0.033$$; $$\rho$$ moves from 0.042 to 0.301 and $$s$$ from 6.38 to 3.17. Separately, one break in the beta, at 2025-07-18, with $$F^{\sup} = 7.23$$ and $$\hat p = 0.028$$; $$\beta_{M}$$ moves from 0.61 ($$t = 3.3$$) to 1.34 ($$t = 3.7$$) and the regression $$R^2$$ from 0.03 to 0.33. No significant break in the Nasdaq-100 correlation ($$\hat p = 0.133$$) or the gold correlation ($$\hat p = 0.203$$); two in the dollar correlation, at 2017-03-31 and 2018-02-02, after which $$\rho = -0.234$$.

**Robustness to crypto's own history.** An obvious objection is that Terra and FTX in 2022, or the spot exchange-traded funds of 2024, are doing work being credited to the Federal Reserve. Adding controls for stablecoin issuance, perpetual funding, on-chain activity and eight dated crypto catastrophes does not overturn the recent result: the equity beta *strengthens*, from 1.26 ($$t = 2.4$$) to 1.58 ($$t = 3.7$$). Three candidate controls were deliberately excluded as transforms of the dependent variable, which would have absorbed it mechanically - the MVRV ratio, whose change correlates 0.93 with the bitcoin return after 2019; the ETH/BTC ratio, which equals $$r^{ETH} - r^{B}$$ by construction; and the crypto market return excluding bitcoin, correlation 0.64. Of the controls that survive, the ETF launch carries a coefficient of $$-4.9\%$$ on weekly returns with $$t = -6.6$$, a textbook case of selling the news.

**Analogue matching.** Each window $$w$$ is summarised by a vector $$f_w$$ of eleven risk characteristics: annualised volatility and Sharpe ratio, skewness, excess kurtosis, maximum drawdown, first-order autocorrelation, share of positive periods, and correlations with equities, gold, the ten-year yield and the dollar. Features are standardised across the panel and similarity is [Mahalanobis distance](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13171-019-00164-5), which discounts the fact that volatility, drawdown and kurtosis largely repeat one another:

$$
d(w, b) \;=\; \sqrt{\left(z_w - z_b\right)' \hat\Sigma^{-1} \left(z_w - z_b\right)},
\qquad z_w = \frac{f_w - \bar f}{s_f} .
$$

Scanning forty thousand windows guarantees a close-looking match, so the distance means nothing without a null. Ours lets randomly drawn windows from the panel search the panel exactly as bitcoin does, inheriting the same multiple-comparison advantage. With $$a_j$$ the asset owning window $$j$$ and $$M$$ pseudo-targets $$w_m$$,

$$
\hat p_{\text{match}} \;=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{ \min_{j \,:\, a_j \neq a_m} d(w_j, w_m) \;\le\; \min_{j} d(w_j, b) \right\} .
$$

This runs from 0.80 to 1.00 in every regime and at both frequencies: bitcoin is never matched closer than chance. Path similarity in the first analogue chart is dynamic time warping on amplitude-normalised cumulative return paths, and a two-sample Wasserstein distance on standardised returns serves as a third independent check; a match is reported only when at least two of the three agree.

**Data.** Bitcoin prices are the [CoinMetrics community](https://docs.coinmetrics.io/) reference rate, daily from July 2010, cross-checked against Bitstamp and Coinbase. The sample starts in January 2013 on empirical grounds rather than convention: median absolute disagreement between independent feeds is 7.0% in 2011 and 1.1% in 2012, but at most 0.3% from 2013 onward, so Mt. Gox-era data will not support beta estimation. Macro series are 88 [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/) series entered as changes, never levels; factors are Fama-French; gold and silver are [LBMA](https://www.lbma.org.uk/prices-and-data/precious-metal-prices) fixes from 1968; national indices are the [OECD monthly share-price family](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SPASTT01TRM661N). Two licensing traps: on FRED's public CSV endpoint every ICE BofA credit series is truncated to a rolling three years and SP500 to ten, so Moody's BAA10Y and the Fama-French market factor carry the long history instead.

Weekly returns are primary because bitcoin trades continuously against a six-and-a-half-hour equity session and calendar-day alignment attenuates betas; [Dimson](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X%2879%2990013-8) lead-lag corrected betas divided by contemporaneous betas run 0.88 to 0.98 after 2020, so the distortion is immaterial in the modern sample. All inference is [Newey-West HAC](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913610). Time-varying betas were estimated three ways - rolling least squares, a [DCC-GARCH](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500102288618487) on normal-score-transformed residuals, and a random-walk-coefficient Kalman filter - which agree on the direction and timing of every major shift but not its amplitude: the DCC estimate of $$\rho$$ spans only 0.12 to 0.24 against the rolling estimate's $$-0.22$$ to $$0.55$$, because correlation targeting makes it a parametric smoother. Any single published correlation number is partly a modelling choice.

**Further caveats.** Keyless sources could not supply delisted single-name equities, so the panel contains no dot-com casualties and its 49 industry portfolios are *diversified*, capping their attainable volatility; this biases the study toward the no-analogue conclusion. The OECD indices are local-currency price indices rather than dollar total returns, which flatters high-inflation countries such as Turkey. Regime boundaries are wide: with roughly 700 weekly observations, confidence intervals on break dates run to months. A placebo test that splits the sample at random dates reproduces the observed spread of Sharpe ratios across regimes ($$\hat p = 0.22$$), so regime-by-regime Sharpe rankings are descriptive only. Every regime fit is in-sample, and none of this is a trading strategy.

**Reproducing it.** All code is at [github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/bitcoin_macro](https://github.com/chandlerzuo/chandlerzuo.github.io/tree/master/codes/bitcoin_macro); no data is committed, and running `./run_all.sh` on a clean checkout downloads every input from keyless public sources and regenerates every figure and table in about thirty-five minutes.
